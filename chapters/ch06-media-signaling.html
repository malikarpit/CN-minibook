Section 6.1

Classification: Guided vs Unguided Transmission Media 🎓 Uni: Transmission Media

A transmission medium is the physical communication path that carries a data signal from a sender to a receiver. Located beneath the Physical Layer, transmission media are fundamentally divided into two major classes based on physical conduit confinement:

Figure 6.1: Taxonomy of Transmission Media
                               TRANSMISSION MEDIA
                                        |
                 +----------------------+----------------------+
                 |                                             |
         GUIDED (Wired / Bounded)                    UNGUIDED (Wireless / Unbounded)
                 |                                             |
        +--------+--------+                           +--------+--------+
        |        |        |                           |        |        |
     Twisted  Coaxial  Optical                      Radio   Microwaves Infrared
      Pair     Cable    Fiber                       Waves      |
     (UTP/STP) (50Ω/75Ω)(SM/MM)                                +-> Terrestrial
                                                               +-> Satellite
Guided vs Unguided Media

• Guided Media (Wired / Bound): Electromagnetic waves are physically constrained and guided along a solid conduit (copper wire or silica glass fiber). Provides high data rates, low interference, and physical security.
• Unguided Media (Wireless / Unbound): Electromagnetic waves radiate freely through the atmosphere, vacuum, or water without physical conductors. Provides device mobility and wide geographical coverage at the cost of vulnerability to attenuation and interference.

Section 6.2

Twisted Pair Cable (UTP & STP)

The Twisted Pair Cable is the most ubiquitous transmission medium in local area networks (LANs). It consists of two insulated copper conductor wires twisted around each other in a regular helical pattern.

Figure 6.2: Why Are the Conductors Twisted?
   EXTERNAL NOISE SOURCE:
           \   |   /   (Electromagnetic Radiation)
            \  |  /
   Wire 1:  ---\     /---\     /---
                \   /     \   /
   Wire 2:  -----/ \-------/ \-----
             [Loop 1]   [Loop 2]

   • In Loop 1: Wire 1 is closer to the noise source -> picks up +ΔV.
   • In Loop 2: Wire 2 is closer to the noise source -> picks up +ΔV.
   • The receiver measures the DIFFERENCE (V1 - V2):
     Differential Voltage = (V_signal + ΔV) - (-V_signal + ΔV) = 2·V_signal!
   👉 The noise term (+ΔV) cancels out completely!
Differential Signaling & Noise Cancellation

Twisting ensures that both wires are equally exposed to ambient electromagnetic noise. Because the receiver measures the voltage difference between the two wires rather than the voltage relative to ground, identical induced noise spikes on both wires cancel each other out mathematically!

UTP vs STP

Feature Unshielded Twisted Pair (UTP) Shielded Twisted Pair (STP)
Shielding Only plastic insulation around each wire and an outer PVC jacket. No metal foil. Individual pairs wrapped in metal foil shielding, plus an overall braided wire shield.
EMI Immunity Moderate (adequate for indoor office environments). High (resists industrial machinery EMI and external radio frequency noise).
Cost & Bulk Inexpensive, thin, lightweight, flexible, and very easy to terminate. Expensive, thick, rigid, heavier, and requires proper electrical grounding.
Connector Standard RJ45 (8P8C) plug. Shielded RJ45 or specialized industrial connectors.

UTP Category Standards (TIA/EIA-568)

Category Rated Bandwidth Maximum Data Rate Typical Modern Application
Cat 3 16 MHz 10 Mbps Legacy 10Base-T Ethernet, analog telephone lines.
Cat 5 100 MHz 100 Mbps (Fast Ethernet) Obsolete 100Base-TX.
Cat 5e 100 MHz 1 Gbps (Gigabit Ethernet) 1000Base-T over 100 meters (reduced crosstalk).
Cat 6 250 MHz 10 Gbps (up to 55m) / 1 Gbps (100m) Standard modern enterprise office LAN cabling.
Cat 6a 500 MHz 10 Gbps over full 100m Datacenter server-to-switch interconnects.
Section 6.3

Coaxial Cable (Coax)

A Coaxial Cable features two concentric conductors sharing the same geometric center axis (hence co-axial). It was the backbone of early Ethernet LANs and remains dominant in cable television and DOCSIS broadband internet.

Figure 6.3: Coaxial Cable Cross-Section
   (Center outward):
   1. [=====] Inner Conductor (Solid or stranded copper wire - carries signal)
   2. [#####] Dielectric Insulator (Non-conductive plastic foam)
   3. [:::::] Outer Metallic Conductor (Braided copper mesh / aluminum foil - ground & shield)
   4. [=====] Outer Protective Plastic Jacket (PVC or Teflon)

Baseband vs Broadband Coaxial Cable

Type Characteristic Impedance Signaling Mode Historical / Modern Use
Baseband Coaxial 50 $\Omega$ (Ohms) Digital signaling; entire cable bandwidth dedicated to a single digital pulse stream at any given time. Legacy 10Base-5 ("Thicknet") and 10Base-2 ("Thinnet") bus Ethernet.
Broadband Coaxial 75 $\Omega$ (Ohms) Analog signaling; frequency division multiplexing (FDM) carries hundreds of TV channels and data streams simultaneously. Cable TV (CATV), DOCSIS residential broadband cable modems.
💡 Connectors for Coaxial Cable

• BNC (Bayonet Neill-Concelman): Classic push-and-twist connector used in legacy 10Base-2 Ethernet and video broadcast equipment.
• BNC T-Connector: Inserted directly into the NIC of a PC to splice into a bus backbone.
• 50 $\Omega$ Terminator: Attached to both physical ends of a coaxial bus to absorb electrical reflections and prevent standing wave collisions.
• F-Type Connector: Threaded screw-on connector standard on modern cable modems and TV set-top boxes.

Section 6.4

Optical Fiber Cable (Physics & Total Internal Reflection)

Optical Fiber is the undisputed king of high-speed long-distance telecommunications. Instead of conducting electrical currents, optical fibers transmit data as high-frequency pulses of light (photons) through thin strands of ultra-pure silica glass ($SiO_2$) or transparent plastic.

Figure 6.4: Total Internal Reflection in Optical Fiber
                    CLADDING (Refractive Index n2)
      ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
      \                                                       /
       \  Angle of Incidence θ_i > Critical Angle θ_c        /
        \                                                   /
         \                                                 /
          ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
                    CORE (Refractive Index n1 > n2)
Total Internal Reflection (TIR) & Snell's Law

For light to remain trapped inside the core and propagate across hundreds of kilometers without escaping into the jacket, two physical conditions must hold:
1. Refractive Index Condition: The core glass must be optically denser than the outer cladding:
n_core (n1) > n_cladding (n2)
2. Critical Angle Condition: The angle of incidence ($\theta_i$) at the core-cladding boundary must be greater than the critical angle ($\theta_c$):
\sin(\theta_c) = \frac{n_2}{n_1}
When $\theta_i > \theta_c$, $100\%$ of the optical energy is reflected back into the core with zero refractive loss!

Single-Mode vs Multi-Mode Fiber

Characteristic Single-Mode Fiber (SMF) Multi-Mode Fiber (MMF)
Core Diameter Extremely narrow: 8 to 10 $\mu\text{m}$ (roughly the width of a human red blood cell). Much wider: 50 to 62.5 $\mu\text{m}$.
Light Propagation Paths Only one single direct ray/mode of light travels straight down the center axis. Zero modal dispersion! Multiple rays/modes bounce off walls at different angles, arriving at slightly different times (modal dispersion).
Light Source Precision Injection Laser Diodes (ILD) (expensive, narrow spectral width). Light Emitting Diodes (LED) or VCSELs (inexpensive).
Maximum Distance Up to 80 to 100+ km without repeaters. Up to 300 to 2000 meters (limited by modal dispersion).
Applications Undersea transoceanic cables, telecom backbones, long-haul inter-city links. Campus LAN backbones, intra-datacenter server racks.

Advantages & Disadvantages of Optical Fiber

  • Enormous Bandwidth: Easily supports data rates from 10 Gbps to 800 Gbps per wavelength.
  • Zero EMI Immunity: Being made of glass, fiber is 100% immune to electromagnetic interference, radio interference, and high-voltage power lines.
  • Extremely Low Attenuation: Signals travel tens of kilometers before requiring regeneration (typically $\approx 0.2\text{ dB/km}$ at 1550 nm).
  • High Security: Extremely difficult to tap without breaking the glass and causing an immediate detectable drop in optical power.
  • Disadvantages: Higher installation cost, fragile (glass can crack if bent beyond minimum bend radius), and splicing requires precision fusion equipment.
Section 6.5

Unguided Media: Radio Waves (3 kHz to 1 GHz)

Radio waves are electromagnetic waves ranging from $3\text{ kHz}$ to $1\text{ GHz}$. They are omnidirectional: when an antenna transmits radio waves, energy radiates outward in all directions equally. This means the transmitting and receiving antennas do not need to be physically aligned.

Omnidirectional Radio Propagation

Radio waves penetrate walls easily (especially lower frequencies), making them ideal for indoor multicast, mobile cellular, and consumer broadcast systems (AM/FM radio, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth). However, omnidirectional propagation means signals are susceptible to interference from other transmitters sharing the same frequency band.

Section 6.6

Unguided Media: Microwaves (1 GHz to 300 GHz)

Electromagnetic waves between $1\text{ GHz}$ and $300\text{ GHz}$ are called microwaves. Unlike radio waves, microwaves are highly unidirectional (directional). Energy can be focused into a narrow, concentrated beam using parabolic dish or horn antennas.

⚠️ The Line-of-Sight Constraint

Microwaves follow strict line-of-sight propagation. They cannot bend around the curvature of the Earth, nor can they pass through mountains or large solid buildings. To establish long-distance terrestrial microwave links, relay towers equipped with parabolic antennas must be erected on hilltops or tall buildings every 40 to 50 km.

Section 6.7

Unguided Media: Infrared (300 GHz to 400 THz)

Infrared waves operate at frequencies from $300\text{ GHz}$ to $400\text{ THz}$ (wavelengths from 1 mm to 770 nm). Infrared communication is strictly short-range and line-of-sight.

  • Cannot Penetrate Solid Walls: This physical limitation is actually a tremendous security advantage! An infrared transmission inside a conference room cannot leak into the next room or be eavesdropped by outsiders.
  • No Government License Required: Because infrared does not interfere with neighboring rooms, it is free from FCC/ITU spectrum licensing.
  • Applications: Television remote controls, wireless keyboards/mice, infrared wireless transceivers.
Section 6.8

Satellite Communication (GEO, MEO, LEO)

A communications satellite acts as an enormous microwave relay repeater in the sky. An earth station transmits a microwave signal on an uplink frequency; the satellite's onboard transponder receives the signal, amplifies it, translates it to a different downlink frequency (to prevent interference between sender and receiver), and beams it back to Earth.

Orbit Type Altitude Above Earth Round-Trip Latency Constellation Size & Applications
GEO (Geostationary Earth Orbit) 35,786 km (Orbital period exactly matches Earth's 24h rotation). High latency: $\approx 250\text{ to } 280\text{ ms}$. Just 3 satellites cover almost the entire globe! Used for satellite television broadcast (DTH) and weather monitoring.
MEO (Medium Earth Orbit) 5,000 to 20,000 km Moderate latency: $50\text{ to } 100\text{ ms}$. Global navigation systems: GPS (USA, ~20,200 km), Galileo (Europe), GLONASS (Russia). Typically 24 satellites.
LEO (Low Earth Orbit) 500 to 1,500 km Low latency: $20\text{ to } 40\text{ ms}$ (comparable to terrestrial fiber!). High-speed satellite broadband internet: Starlink, OneWeb. Requires thousands of fast-moving satellites.

Section 6.10

Digital-to-Digital: Line Coding Fundamentals 🎓 Uni: Signaling & Line Coding

How do we represent digital computer bits ($1$ and $0$) as electrical voltages on a copper wire? The naive assumption is simply to use zero volts ($0\text{V}$) for bit $0$ and five volts ($+5\text{V}$) for bit $1$. In real-world engineering, this primitive approach fails catastrophically due to three major electrical hazards:

The Three Critical Challenges of Line Coding

1. DC Component (Direct Current Bias): When voltage levels do not average out to zero over time, a non-zero average voltage accumulates on the cable. Transformers, capacitors, and AC-coupled communication equipment block DC signals, leading to signal distortion and wasted power.
2. Baseline Wandering: A receiver determines whether a bit is $1$ or $0$ by comparing the signal voltage against a running average threshold (the baseline). Long consecutive strings of identical bits (all $1\text{s}$ or all $0\text{s}$) drift the baseline, causing the receiver to misinterpret incoming bits.
3. Clock Synchronization Loss: When transmitting a long string of continuous identical bits (e.g., twenty consecutive zeros), the voltage line remains flat. The receiver's internal clock drifts out of sync with the sender's clock, losing count of how many bits were received!

Section 6.11

Non-Return-to-Zero (NRZ-L & NRZ-I)

In NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero) schemes, the electrical signal maintains a constant voltage throughout the entire duration of the bit interval; the signal never returns to zero volts in the middle of a bit.

NRZ-L vs NRZ-I

• NRZ-L (NRZ-Level): The voltage level depends directly on the value of the bit:
  - Bit $0$ = Positive Voltage ($+V$)
  - Bit $1$ = Negative Voltage ($-V$)
• NRZ-I (NRZ-Invert): The signal inverts (transitions) if the bit is a $1$; it makes no transition if the bit is a $0$:
  - Bit $1$ = Transition at beginning of bit interval.
  - Bit $0$ = No transition at beginning of bit interval.

Figure 6.5: NRZ-L vs NRZ-I for Bit Stream [ 1 0 1 1 0 0 1 0 ]
   Bit Stream:   |  1  |  0  |  1  |  1  |  0  |  0  |  1  |  0  |
   -----------------------------------------------------------------
   NRZ-L:
   +V            |     |-----|     |     |-----|-----|     |-----|
   -V            |-----|     |-----|-----|     |     |-----|     |

   NRZ-I:
   +V            |-----|-----|     |-----|-----|-----|     |     |
   -V            |     |     |-----|     |     |     |-----|-----|
   (Transition at every '1'; maintains level on '0')
⚠️ Limitations of NRZ

While NRZ is simple and bandwidth-efficient, it suffers from two major flaws:
• NRZ-L loses clock synchronization on long runs of consecutive $0\text{s}$ or $1\text{s}$.
• NRZ-I fixes long runs of $1\text{s}$ (because each $1$ forces a transition), but it still loses synchronization on long runs of $0\text{s}$!

Section 6.12

Manchester Encoding (IEEE 802.3 Standard)

To eliminate both the DC component and clock synchronization loss completely, Manchester Encoding introduces a mandatory voltage transition in the exact middle of every single bit interval.

Manchester Encoding Rules (IEEE 802.3 Standard)

Every bit period is split into two equal halves with a mid-bit transition:
• Bit 0: High-to-Low transition (High first half, Low second half).
• Bit 1: Low-to-High transition (Low first half, High second half).
Note: The mid-bit transition serves two purposes simultaneously: 1. It carries the data value, and 2. It acts as an embedded self-clocking strobe!

Figure 6.6: Manchester Waveform for [ 1 0 1 1 0 0 1 0 ]
   Bit Stream:   |   1   |   0   |   1   |   1   |   0   |   0   |   1   |   0   |
   ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   +V            |   /---|---|   |   /---|   /---|---|   |---|   |   /---|---|   |
   -V            |---/   |   |---|---/   |---/   |   |---|   |---|---/   |   |---|
                 (L -> H)|(H -> L)|(L -> H)|(L -> H)|(H -> L)|(H -> L)|(L -> H)|(H -> L)
💡 The Price of Manchester: Double the Bandwidth!

Because there is guaranteed to be at least one transition per bit (and up to two transitions when alternating), the minimum bandwidth of Manchester is double that of NRZ! For a 10 Mbps Ethernet link using Manchester encoding, the physical signaling rate is 20 Megabaud. This is why Fast Ethernet (100 Mbps) replaced Manchester with 4B/5B + MLT-3 to conserve bandwidth.

Section 6.13

Differential Manchester Encoding (Token Ring IEEE 802.5)

Differential Manchester combines the self-clocking benefits of Manchester with the differential transition logic of NRZ-I. It is famously used in Token Ring LANs (IEEE 802.5).

Differential Manchester Rules

1. Mid-bit Transition: A transition ALWAYS occurs in the middle of every bit interval (used solely for clock synchronization).
2. Beginning-of-bit Transition dictates the Data:
• Bit 0: A transition occurs at the beginning of the bit interval.
• Bit 1: NO transition occurs at the beginning of the bit interval.

Figure 6.7: Differential Manchester Waveform
   Bit Stream:        |   1   |   0   |   1   |   0   |
   -------------------------------------------------------
   Beginning Transition? None     YES      None    YES
   Mid-bit Transition?   ALWAYS   ALWAYS   ALWAYS  ALWAYS
💡 Why Differential Manchester Is Superior in the Field

Differential Manchester depends on transitions rather than absolute voltage levels (+V or -V). If a network technician accidentally swaps the two wires of a twisted-pair cable (polarity reversal), standard Manchester will invert every single bit (turning all $0\text{s}$ into $1\text{s}$ and crashing the link). Differential Manchester is completely immune to wire inversion!

Section 6.15

Chapter Summary & High-Yield Exam Traps

⚡ High-Yield Rapid Review Points
  • Twisted Pair: Twisting cancels induced EMI through differential signaling. UTP (cheap) vs STP (shielded). Cat5e = 1 Gbps; Cat6 = 10 Gbps up to 55m.
  • Coaxial: Inner conductor, dielectric, outer mesh shield, jacket. 50 $\Omega$ Baseband (digital, 10Base2/5); 75 $\Omega$ Broadband (cable TV).
  • Optical Fiber: Operates by Total Internal Reflection (TIR). Condition 1: $n_{\text{core}} > n_{\text{cladding}}$. Condition 2: $\theta_i > \theta_c$.
  • Single-Mode vs Multi-Mode: SMF has tiny core (8–10 $\mu\text{m}$), uses laser, zero modal dispersion, reaches 100 km. MMF has wide core (50–62.5 $\mu\text{m}$), uses LED, reaches 2 km.
  • Unguided Media: Radio ($3\text{ kHz}-1\text{ GHz}$, omnidirectional, penetrates walls); Microwave ($1-300\text{ GHz}$, unidirectional, line-of-sight); Infrared ($300\text{ GHz}-400\text{ THz}$, short-range, blocked by walls).
  • Line Coding:
    • NRZ-L: Bit $0 = +V$, Bit $1 = -V$.
    • NRZ-I: Transition on $1$; no transition on $0$.
    • Manchester: Mid-bit transition always. IEEE 802.3: Bit $0 = \text{High-to-Low}$; Bit $1 = \text{Low-to-High}$.
    • Differential Manchester: Mid-bit transition always. Bit $0 = \text{transition at start}$; Bit $1 = \text{no transition at start}$.

Top 5 High-Yield Traps in Exams

Trap # The Common Misconception The Technical Reality
Trap 1 "Light moves faster in glass than in vacuum." False! Light speed in vacuum is $c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$. Inside silica glass fiber ($n \approx 1.5$), speed is slowed to $v = c/n \approx 2 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ (approx. $5\ \mu\text{s/km}$ delay).
Trap 2 "In Differential Manchester, bit 1 has a transition at the beginning." False! Bit 0 has a transition at the beginning; Bit 1 has NO transition at the beginning. (Both have a transition in the middle).
Trap 3 "Single-mode fiber is called single-mode because it only carries one wavelength of light." False! It is called single-mode because light travels along a single spatial electromagnetic path (zero bounce angles). Through Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM), a single-mode fiber can carry dozens of wavelengths simultaneously!
Trap 4 "NRZ-I solves all synchronization issues." False! NRZ-I solves sync only for strings of $1\text{s}$. If a string of twenty $0\text{s}$ arrives, the voltage remains flat and synchronization is lost!
Trap 5 "Manchester encoding has higher data rate than NRZ." False! Manchester has higher baud rate (signaling transitions), which means it requires twice the channel bandwidth to carry the exact same bit rate as NRZ.
