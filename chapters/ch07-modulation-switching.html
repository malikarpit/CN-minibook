Section 7.1

Why Modulation? (Baseband vs Passband Transmission) 🎓 Uni: Modulation & Demodulation

In Chapter 6, we studied Baseband Transmission: sending digital pulses directly over a cable (e.g., Ethernet over UTP). However, baseband transmission requires a low-pass channel with a spectrum starting near $0\text{ Hz}$. Many critical media—such as the wireless air atmosphere, satellite links, and cable TV networks—are bandpass channels that cannot propagate low-frequency baseband signals. To transmit digital information over these media, we must perform Digital Modulation (Passband Transmission).

Digital-to-Analog Modulation

The process of systematically altering one or more fundamental characteristics (Amplitude, Frequency, or Phase) of a high-frequency analog carrier signal according to the digital binary data bits being transmitted.

The Three Imperative Reasons for Modulation

  1. Antenna Practicality ($\lambda/4$ Rule): Electromagnetic physics dictates that an efficient transmitting antenna must have a physical length equal to at least one-quarter of the signal's wavelength: $$\text{Length} \ge \frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{c}{4f}$$ If you tried to transmit human baseband voice ($f \approx 3\text{ kHz}$) directly through the air, the required antenna would be: $$\text{Length} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{4 \times 3000} = \mathbf{25,000\text{ meters}}\ (25\text{ kilometers tall!})$$ By modulating the data onto a high-frequency carrier (e.g., $f_c = 2.4\text{ GHz}$ for Wi-Fi), the antenna shrinks to just $3.1\text{ centimeters}$, fitting easily inside a smartphone!
  2. Frequency Multiplexing: Modulation shifts baseband signals to different distinct carrier frequencies, allowing thousands of radio stations, TV channels, or cellular calls to share the air simultaneously without collision.
  3. Noise Immunity & Range: High-frequency modulated carriers penetrate the atmosphere with far less power loss than low-frequency baseband signals.
Section 7.2

Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK & OOK)

In Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK), the amplitude of the high-frequency carrier wave is varied to represent binary $1\text{s}$ and $0\text{s}$, while the carrier's frequency and phase remain strictly constant.

Binary ASK / On-Off Keying (OOK)

The simplest form of ASK is On-Off Keying (OOK):
• Bit 1: Transmit carrier wave at full amplitude $A$: $s(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t)$
• Bit 0: Transmit zero carrier wave (silence): $s(t) = 0$

Figure 7.1: ASK (OOK) Waveform for Bit Sequence [ 1 0 1 1 0 ]
   Bit Stream: |   1   |   0   |   1   |   1   |   0   |
   -----------------------------------------------------
   Carrier:    |/\/\/\/|       |/\/\/\/|/\/\/\/|       |
               |/\/\/\/|       |/\/\/\/|/\/\/\/|       |
               (Wave)   (Quiet) (Wave)  (Wave)  (Quiet)
⚠️ The Crippling Weakness of ASK: Noise Susceptibility

Natural and man-made electrical noise (lightning, sparks, atmospheric attenuation) primarily affects the amplitude of an electromagnetic wave. Because ASK encodes data in amplitude variations, noise easily alters the signal level, causing bit errors. As a result, pure ASK is rarely used in wireless communications today, though it remains widely used in optical fiber links (laser light turned ON/OFF).

Section 7.3

Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)

In Frequency Shift Keying (FSK), the frequency of the carrier signal is shifted between two distinct values to represent binary $1$ and $0$, while peak amplitude and phase remain constant.

Binary FSK (BFSK) Rules

• Bit 1 (Mark frequency $f_1$): Higher frequency carrier wave.
• Bit 0 (Space frequency $f_2$): Lower frequency carrier wave.
Because data is encoded in frequency rather than amplitude, FSK is far more immune to noise and amplitude fluctuations than ASK!

Figure 7.2: BFSK Waveform for Bit Sequence [ 1 0 1 0 ]
   Bit Stream: |    1    |    0    |    1    |    0    |
   -------------------------------------------------------
   Waveform:   |/\/\/\/\/|  /\  /\ |/\/\/\/\/|  /\  /\ |
               (Fast f1) |(Slow f2)|(Fast f1)|(Slow f2)|

Applications: Legacy 1200 bps dial-up modems (Bell 103/202 standard), caller ID signaling on analog phone lines, and low-power HF radio.

Section 7.4

Phase Shift Keying (PSK & BPSK)

In Phase Shift Keying (PSK), the phase of the carrier signal is shifted to represent binary values, while amplitude and frequency remain constant. PSK is significantly more bandwidth-efficient than FSK and vastly more noise-resistant than ASK.

Binary PSK (BPSK) Rules

Uses two opposite phases separated by $180^\circ$ ($\pi$ radians):
• Bit 0: Phase $= 0^\circ \implies s(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t)$
• Bit 1: Phase $= 180^\circ \implies s(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t + \pi) = -A \cos(2\pi f_c t)$
Whenever the bit stream switches between 0 and 1, the waveform instantaneously flips upside down!

Figure 7.3: BPSK Constellation Diagram
                        Quadrature (Q)
                              ^
                              |
             Bit 1            |            Bit 0
           (-A, 0)            |           (+A, 0)
              *---------------+---------------* ===> In-Phase (I)
           (180°)             |              (0°)
                              |
                              |
Section 7.5

Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK)

In BPSK, each symbol transmits only 1 single bit. Can we transmit more bits per symbol to double our spectral efficiency? Yes! Quadrature PSK (QPSK) uses four distinct phase shifts separated by $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$ radians).

QPSK Symbol Mapping (2 Bits Per Symbol)

Because there are $L = 4$ distinct phase states, each symbol carries $\log_2(4) = \mathbf{2\text{ bits}}$ (called a dibit):
• Phase $45^\circ$: Represents bit pair 11
• Phase $135^\circ$: Represents bit pair 01
• Phase $225^\circ$: Represents bit pair 00
• Phase $315^\circ$: Represents bit pair 10
For the exact same channel bandwidth, QPSK delivers DOUBLE the data rate of BPSK!

Figure 7.4: QPSK Constellation Diagram (4 Phase Points)
                        Quadrature (Q)
                              ^
                 01 (*135°)   |   11 (*45°)
                              |
             -----------------+----------------- > In-Phase (I)
                              |
                 00 (*225°)   |   10 (*315°)
                              |
Section 7.6

Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM)

If we try to squeeze more than 8 or 16 phase shifts into pure PSK (e.g., 32-PSK), the constellation points crowd so tightly together around the unit circle that even tiny noise fluctuations cause phase detection errors. To overcome this limitation, Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) combines both ASK (Amplitude Variation) and PSK (Phase Variation) simultaneously.

QAM Constellations

In QAM, two carrier waves of the same frequency but shifted by $90^\circ$ (In-phase $I$ and Quadrature $Q$) are independently amplitude-modulated and summed together. This creates a 2D rectangular grid of distinct constellation points:
• 16-QAM: 16 constellation points $\implies \log_2(16) = \mathbf{4\text{ bits per symbol}}$
• 64-QAM: 64 constellation points $\implies \log_2(64) = \mathbf{6\text{ bits per symbol}}$
• 256-QAM: 256 constellation points $\implies \log_2(256) = \mathbf{8\text{ bits per symbol}}$
• 1024-QAM / 4096-QAM: Standard in modern Wi-Fi 6/7 and 5G cellular networks!

Figure 7.5: 16-QAM Constellation Diagram (4x4 Grid = 16 Points)
                        Quadrature (Q)
                              ^
                    *    *    |    *    *
                    *    *    |    *    *
             -----------------+----------------- > In-Phase (I)
                    *    *    |    *    *
                    *    *    |    *    *
                              |
             Each point carries 4 bits! Example: (0110)

Section 7.8

Why Multiplexing?

In any wide-area network, laying physical transmission media (such as undersea transoceanic fiber or burying copper cables across cities) is enormously expensive. If each individual telephone conversation or data connection required its own dedicated physical wire across the country, telecommunications would be financially impossible.

Multiplexing

The set of telecommunication techniques that allows the simultaneous transmission of multiple independent data streams or voice calls over a single shared high-capacity physical communication channel (link).
• Multiplexer (MUX): Combines $N$ input communication channels into 1 shared composite channel at the sender.
• Demultiplexer (DEMUX): Separates the composite signal back into $N$ individual streams at the destination.

Section 7.9

Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM)

Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM) is an analog technique used when the total bandwidth of the physical link is significantly larger than the bandwidth required by each individual channel.

Figure 7.6: FDM Spectrum Allocation with Guard Bands
   Total Physical Link Bandwidth
   |========================================================================|
   [ Channel 1 ] |Guard| [ Channel 2 ] |Guard| [ Channel 3 ] |Guard| [ Chan 4 ]
   (e.g., 90.1 MHz) Band  (e.g., 93.5 MHz) Band  (e.g., 98.3 MHz) Band  (104 MHz)
Guard Bands in FDM

Unused narrow frequency intervals left between adjacent channel allocations to prevent overlapping and eliminate mutual electromagnetic crosstalk caused by non-ideal filter roll-off.

Applications: AM and FM radio broadcasting, commercial cable television (carrying hundreds of channels simultaneously over one 75 $\Omega$ coaxial cable), and cellular voice systems (FDMA).

Section 7.10

Time Division Multiplexing (TDM: Synchronous vs Statistical)

Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) is a digital technique where multiple digital streams share a single communication line by interleaving portions of each signal in the time domain. The entire channel bandwidth is dedicated to one single connection for a brief fraction of time (called a time slot).

Characteristic Synchronous TDM Statistical (Asynchronous) TDM
Time Slot Allocation Pre-allocated, fixed time slots in round-robin order to every connected input line, regardless of whether that line has active data to send. Dynamic on-demand allocation; time slots are allocated exclusively to channels that currently have active data in their input buffer.
Empty Slot Waste ⚠️ If Channel B is silent, its allocated time slot travels across the wire completely empty, wasting bandwidth! ✅ Zero empty slots! Silent channels consume zero time slots on the shared link.
Addressing Required? ❌ No. Slot position itself identifies the channel (Slot 1 = User 1). ✅ Yes! Because slots arrive out of fixed order, each slot must carry an address/ID header identifying the source channel.
Typical Use Traditional digital telephone multiplexing: T1 Carrier (USA, 1.544 Mbps, 24 voice channels), E1 Carrier (Europe, 2.048 Mbps, 30 channels). Modern packet switches, asynchronous terminal multiplexers, IP routers.
Section 7.11

Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM & DWDM)

Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) is conceptually identical to FDM, but designed specifically for optical fiber cables. Multiple laser light beams of different optical wavelengths (different colors/frequencies) are combined and propagated through a single strand of silica glass fiber.

Figure 7.7: Wavelength Division Multiplexing Concept
   Laser 1 (λ1 = 1550 nm) ---\                              /---> Detector 1 (λ1)
   Laser 2 (λ2 = 1552 nm) ----+== PRISM / MUX ==> [ FIBER ] ==+== DEMUX ==> Detector 2 (λ2)
   Laser 3 (λ3 = 1554 nm) ---/                              \---> Detector 3 (λ3)
DWDM (Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing)

By spacing optical wavelengths extremely close together (channel spacing of $0.8\text{ nm}$ or $0.4\text{ nm}$ in the 1550 nm window), modern DWDM multiplexes 80 to 160+ independent 100 Gbps wavelengths onto a single hair-thin optical fiber, achieving aggregate data rates exceeding 16 Terabits per second (Tbps) per fiber strand!

Section 7.13

Why Switching?

If we want to connect $N$ computers or telephones worldwide, building direct point-to-point dedicated links between every pair requires a full mesh topology with:

$$\text{Number of Links} = \frac{N(N - 1)}{2}$$

For just 1 million users, this requires roughly 500 billion physical cables—an engineering impossibility. A Switched Network solves this by interconnecting devices through a series of intermediate nodes called Switches.

Section 7.14

Circuit Switching

Circuit Switching was invented for the traditional Public Switched Telephone Network (PSTN). It establishes a dedicated, physical end-to-end communication path between sender and receiver before any data can be transferred.

The Three Mandatory Phases of Circuit Switching

1. Circuit Establishment (Setup Phase): A signaling message traverses the network from caller to callee, reserving dedicated physical switches, copper circuits, and channel bandwidth along the entire route. If any intermediate switch has no free lines, a "busy signal" is returned.
2. Data Transfer Phase: Continuous analog or digital data flows across the reserved dedicated pipe with zero queuing delay and constant propagation latency.
3. Circuit Teardown (Release Phase): When either party terminates the session, a teardown signal is propagated to release all reserved switches and link bandwidth back to the shared pool.

⚠️ The Flaw of Circuit Switching: Resource Inefficiency

Once a circuit is established, that physical bandwidth is exclusively reserved for those two users, even if neither person is speaking! During silent pauses in a phone call or while a human is reading a web page, the reserved bandwidth sits completely idle and cannot be utilized by any other waiting callers.

Section 7.15

Packet Switching (Store-and-Forward) 🎓 Uni: Packet Switching

To eliminate the massive resource waste of circuit switching, computer scientists developed Packet Switching—the foundational switching architecture of the modern Internet.

Packet Switching Principle

Long application messages are broken down into small, manageable discrete chunks called Packets. Each packet contains a portion of the data payload plus a header with source and destination addresses. Packets are routed dynamically across intermediate routers using the Store-and-Forward mechanism.

Figure 7.8: Store-and-Forward Packet Transmission Pipelining
   Host A ----(Link 1: R bps)----> Router 1 ----(Link 2: R bps)----> Host B
   
   • Packet 1 transmitted onto Link 1.
   • Router 1 MUST completely receive and buffer all bits of Packet 1 before
     forwarding it onto Link 2!
   • While Router 1 forwards Packet 1 onto Link 2, Host A transmits Packet 2 onto Link 1!
   👉 Transmission is pipelined, dramatically reducing total end-to-end delivery time!
Section 7.16

Datagram Networks vs Virtual Circuit Networks

Within packet switching, there are two distinct architectural philosophies: Datagram (Connectionless) and Virtual Circuit (Connection-Oriented).

Comparison Parameter Datagram Packet Switching (e.g., Internet IP) Virtual Circuit Packet Switching (e.g., ATM, Frame Relay)
Connection Setup Zero setup phase. Packets can be injected into the network immediately. Mandatory VC setup phase. Virtual circuit must be established before sending data.
Packet Routing & Paths Each packet is treated as an independent datagram. Intermediate routers evaluate routing tables dynamically; packets may take completely different physical paths! All packets in a connection follow the exact same pre-established virtual route.
Packet Ordering Packets may arrive at destination out-of-order (must be re-sequenced by Layer 4 TCP). Packets arrive in strict sequential order.
Header Overhead Large header: Every single packet must carry full 32-bit/128-bit global Source and Destination IP addresses. Tiny header: Packets carry only a short Virtual Circuit Identifier (VCI) (e.g., 16 or 24 bits).
Router State & Memory Stateless routers: Routers maintain no memory of active user connections. Stateful routers: Routers must maintain a VCI translation table for every active connection.
Failure Behavior Highly robust: If a router crashes, subsequent datagrams automatically route around the failure. Vulnerable: If any intermediate router along the VC crashes, the entire connection is terminated and must be re-established.
Section 7.17

Message Switching (Historical Context)

In historical Message Switching (used in early telegraph and military telex networks), the sender transmits the entire message as a single complete unit (without breaking it into packets). Intermediate switches store the entire message on hard disks before forwarding it to the next switch (often called Store-and-Forward Telex).

  • Massive Buffering Required: If a message is 50 MB, every intermediate switch requires 50 MB of RAM/disk space.
  • No Pipelining: An entire multi-megabyte message must completely cross Link 1 before Link 2 can even begin transmitting, causing enormous delays.
  • Obsolete: Completely replaced by packet switching.

Section 7.19

Chapter Summary & High-Yield Exam Traps

⚡ High-Yield Rapid Review Points
  • Antenna Formula: Length $\ge \lambda/4 = c/(4f)$. Modulation shifts signals to high frequency so antennas become small and practical.
  • Modulation Schemes:
    • ASK: Changes amplitude. Sensitive to noise. (Used in fiber OOK).
    • FSK: Changes frequency. Good noise immunity. (1200 bps modems).
    • BPSK: $180^\circ$ phase shift. 1 bit/symbol. Excellent noise immunity.
    • QPSK: 4 phase shifts ($90^\circ$). 2 bits/symbol. Doubles bit rate for same bandwidth.
    • QAM: Combines ASK + PSK. $r = \log_2(M)$ bits/symbol. (Wi-Fi 6, 5G, cable modems).
  • Multiplexing Schemes:
    • FDM: Analog. Frequency spectrum divided with guard bands. (Radio, CATV).
    • Synchronous TDM: Digital. Fixed time slots. Silent channels waste slots. (T1/E1).
    • Statistical TDM: Digital. On-demand time slots with addressing. Zero waste.
    • WDM / DWDM: Optical. Wavelengths of light in glass fiber. (Terabit backbones).
  • Switching Schemes:
    • Circuit Switching: Dedicated physical path, 3 phases (setup/transfer/teardown), constant latency, wasted bandwidth.
    • Datagram Packet Switching: Connectionless, independent routing, out-of-order arrival, robust against failure, powers the Internet.
    • Virtual Circuit Packet Switching: Connection-oriented, fixed route, VCI headers, strict ordering.

Top 5 High-Yield Traps in Exams

Trap # The Common Misconception The Technical Reality
Trap 1 "Virtual Circuit switching reserves a dedicated physical wire." False! Virtual circuit switching is still packet switching. Physical links are shared via statistical multiplexing; only the logical routing path and VCI translation table entries are pre-established!
Trap 2 "In Synchronous TDM, if a sender has no data, the multiplexer skips that slot." False! Synchronous TDM assigns rigid, fixed slots. If a sender is idle, its time slot is transmitted completely empty across the link! Only Statistical TDM skips idle channels.
Trap 3 "16-QAM requires four times the bandwidth of BPSK." False! 16-QAM carries 4 bits per symbol, so it achieves four times the bit rate within the EXACT SAME bandwidth as BPSK!
Trap 4 "FDM requires digital-to-analog converters." False! FDM is a strictly analog multiplexing technique that modulates analog subcarriers. TDM is the primary digital multiplexing technique.
Trap 5 "Internet IP packets travel via Virtual Circuits." False! The Internet Protocol (IP) operates strictly as a Datagram Network. Every IP datagram is routed independently by intermediate routers.
