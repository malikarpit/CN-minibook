7.1 Why Modulation? (Baseband vs. Passband)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Physical Layer: Modulation Need (Baseband vs Passband Channels)
Modulation — Definition & Core Objective
Modulation is the systematic modification of one or more fundamental characteristics (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a high-frequency analog carrier wave in accordance with the instantaneous values of a digital or analog information-bearing signal (the baseband modulating signal).

Baseband vs. Passband Transmission

  • Baseband Transmission: Sending a digital signal directly over a channel without frequency shifting. Requires a low-pass channel (a physical wire or optical fiber with bandwidth starting from $0\ \text{Hz}$). Impossible over wireless radio or bandpass media.
  • Passband (Broadband) Transmission: Shifting the baseband signal up to a higher frequency band around a carrier frequency $f_c$. Required whenever the physical medium is a bandpass channel (air, satellite, cable TV coaxial systems, or telephone local loops).

The Antenna Length Physics Derivation

In wireless communications, electromagnetic waves radiate efficiently only when the physical transmitting and receiving antenna length $L$ is on the order of a quarter of the signal wavelength ($\lambda / 4$):

$$L = \frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{c}{4f}$$

where $c \approx 3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ is the speed of light, and $f$ is the signal frequency.

Why Modulation is Physically Mandatory

   SCENARIO 1: Direct Baseband Transmission of Voice (f = 3 kHz)
   λ = c / f = (3 × 10^8 m/s) / 3,000 Hz = 100,000 meters = 100 km!
   Antenna Length L = λ / 4 = 100 km / 4 = 25 KILOMETERS!
   (A 25 km antenna is physically and economically impossible!)

   SCENARIO 2: Modulating Voice onto a 5G / Wi-Fi Carrier (f = 3 GHz)
   λ = c / f = (3 × 10^8 m/s) / (3 × 10^9 Hz) = 0.1 meters = 10 cm!
   Antenna Length L = λ / 4 = 10 cm / 4 = 2.5 CENTIMETERS!
   (Easily fits inside a sleek smartphone!)

7.2 Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK & OOK)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Digital Modulation — Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK & OOK)
Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) — Definition
In Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK), the amplitude of the high-frequency analog carrier wave is varied to represent binary data, while its frequency and phase remain strictly constant: $$s(t) = \begin{cases} A_1 \sin(2\pi f_c t) & \text{for bit } 1 \\ A_2 \sin(2\pi f_c t) & \text{for bit } 0 \end{cases}$$ When $A_2 = 0$ (the carrier is simply turned on and off), it is called On-Off Keying (OOK).

Bandwidth Requirement & Severe Limitation

  • Required Bandwidth: $B = (1 + d) \times S$, where $S$ is the baud rate and $d$ is a factor related to modulation filtering ($0 \le d \le 1$).
  • Severe Limitation: Highly susceptible to noise. Transmission impairments (thermal noise, atmospheric interference, line resistance) affect signal amplitude first and foremost. Consequently, pure ASK is rarely used over long-distance radio channels, but remains standard in optical fiber (where lasers are pulsed on and off).

7.3 Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Digital Modulation — Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)
Frequency Shift Keying (FSK) — Definition
In Binary Frequency Shift Keying (BFSK), the frequency of the carrier wave is shifted between two distinct values ($f_1$ and $f_2$) to represent bits $1$ and $0$, while the amplitude and phase remain constant: $$s(t) = \begin{cases} A \sin(2\pi f_1 t) & \text{for bit } 1 \text{ (Mark Frequency)} \\ A \sin(2\pi f_2 t) & \text{for bit } 0 \text{ (Space Frequency)} \end{cases}$$

Bandwidth & Noise Immunity

  • Noise Immunity: Significantly higher than ASK because noise spikes corrupt amplitude, whereas the receiver detects zero-crossings and frequency transitions.
  • Bandwidth Overhead: Requires larger bandwidth than ASK because it must encompass both carrier frequencies plus their sidebands: $$B = 2\Delta f + (1 + d)S$$ where $\Delta f = |f_1 - f_2| / 2$ is the frequency deviation.

7.4 Phase Shift Keying (PSK & BPSK)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Digital Modulation — Phase Shift Keying (BPSK & Constellations)
Binary Phase Shift Keying (BPSK) — Definition
In Binary Phase Shift Keying (BPSK), the phase of the carrier is shifted by $180^\circ$ ($\pi$ radians) to differentiate between binary $1$ and binary $0$, while amplitude and frequency remain constant: $$s(t) = \begin{cases} A \sin(2\pi f_c t) & \text{for bit } 1 \ (\text{Phase } 0^\circ) \\ A \sin(2\pi f_c t + 180^\circ) = -A \sin(2\pi f_c t) & \text{for bit } 0 \ (\text{Phase } 180^\circ) \end{cases}$$

Constellation Diagram for BPSK

A constellation diagram represents modulated symbols in a 2D complex plane, where the horizontal axis represents the In-phase (I) component and the vertical axis represents the Quadrature (Q) component:

BPSK Constellation Diagram

                     Quadrature (Q)
                          │
                          │
     Bit 0                │                Bit 1
     (Phase 180°)         │                (Phase 0°)
    ──────●───────────────┼───────────────●────── In-phase (I)

7.5 Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Digital Modulation — Quadrature PSK (QPSK 4-Phase Modulation)
Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK) — Definition
QPSK modulates two carrier waves in quadrature (sine and cosine) with four distinct phase shifts ($45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ, 315^\circ$), allowing each transmitted symbol to encode two binary data bits ($r = \log_2 4 = 2\ \text{bits/symbol}$): $$\text{Bit Pairs (Dibits): } \{00, 01, 10, 11\}$$

                    ●           │           ●
                                │
           ─────────────────────┼───────────────────── In-phase (I)
                                │
                    ●           │           ●
               11 (225°)        │        10 (315°)
                                │
         Baud Rate S = N / 2. Delivers DOUBLE the bit rate of BPSK in the same bandwidth!

7.6 Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Digital Modulation — Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM Constellations)
Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) — Definition
QAM is an advanced hybrid modulation scheme that simultaneously varies both the amplitude and the phase of two orthogonal carrier waves (cosine and sine): $$s(t) = I(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) - Q(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t)$$ By combining multiple amplitudes with multiple phase shifts, QAM packs vast numbers of bits into each symbol interval.

QAM Constellation Orders & Bit Capacities

QAM Architecture Constellation Points ($M$) Bits per Symbol ($r = \log_2 M$) Primary Application
16-QAM $16$ $4\ \text{bits/symbol}$ 4G LTE cellular, 802.11a/g Wi-Fi.
64-QAM $64$ $6\ \text{bits/symbol}$ DOCSIS 3.0 cable modems, 802.11n Wi-Fi 4.
256-QAM $256$ $8\ \text{bits/symbol}$ DOCSIS 3.1 cable, 802.11ac Wi-Fi 5.
1024-QAM / 4096-QAM $1024 / 4096$ $10 / 12\ \text{bits/symbol}$ Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax), Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be), 5G NR.
💡
The Engineering Trade-Off: Spectral Efficiency vs. Noise Sensitivity Higher-order QAM (like 256-QAM) packs constellation points extremely close together. Even minor thermal noise or phase jitter can push a received symbol into an adjacent decision boundary, causing decoding errors. Therefore, modern systems (such as LTE and Wi-Fi) use Adaptive Modulation and Coding (AMC): switching to 256-QAM when close to the router, and downshifting to QPSK when signal quality drops!

7.8 Why Multiplexing? (Resource Optimization)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Multiplexing Fundamentals — Channel Sharing & Trunk Optimization
Multiplexing — Definition
Multiplexing is the telecommunications technique that combines multiple independent low-speed information streams into a single composite signal transmitted across a shared, high-capacity physical transmission link (trunk). At the destination, a Demultiplexer (DEMUX) separates the composite signal back into its constituent individual streams.

Why It Exists: The Economics of Telecommunication Trunks

Running separate, dedicated physical cables between every pair of communicating cities or continents is economically and physically unfeasible. Optical fibers and coaxial cables possess terabits of bandwidth, whereas individual telephone calls require only 64 kbps. Multiplexing bridges this gap by aggregating thousands of user sessions over one shared medium.

7.9 Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Multiplexing Techniques — Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM & Guard Bands)
Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM) — Definition
FDM is an analog multiplexing technique applied when the total bandwidth of the physical link exceeds the combined bandwidths of the individual signals. The link bandwidth is partitioned into discrete, non-overlapping frequency bands, each modulated by a distinct carrier wave.

The Crucial Role of Guard Bands

Real-world analog bandpass filters have finite roll-off slopes; they cannot cut off frequencies with infinite sharpness. To prevent spectral overlap and crosstalk between adjacent channels, narrow unused frequency intervals called guard bands are inserted between channels.

7.10 Time Division Multiplexing (Synchronous vs. Statistical)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Multiplexing Techniques — Time Division Multiplexing (TDM Variants)
Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) — Definition
TDM is a digital multiplexing mechanism in which multiple low-speed digital connections share the entire transmission medium bandwidth by alternating in time, each connection occupying a periodic time slot within a repeating TDM frame.

Synchronous TDM vs. Statistical (Asynchronous) TDM

Feature Synchronous TDM Statistical / Asynchronous TDM (STDM)
Slot Allocation Fixed & Static. Every input line has an assigned time slot in every frame, regardless of data availability. Dynamic & On-Demand. Slots are allocated only to lines that currently have data in their buffers.
Idle Capacity If a terminal is idle, its time slot goes empty/wasted on the wire. Zero waste. Idle lines receive zero slots; available bandwidth is utilized 100%.
Slot Addressing None needed. Position in the frame identifies the sender implicitly. Mandatory header. Each slot must include an address tag identifying which channel it belongs to.
Aggregate Capacity Medium capacity MUST equal the sum of all input line capacities. Medium capacity can be substantially less than the sum of input capacities (statistical multiplexing).

7.11 Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM & DWDM)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Multiplexing Techniques — Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM/DWDM)
WDM — Optical Domain Multiplexing
Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) is analog frequency-division multiplexing applied in the optical domain. Multiple laser beams of different optical wavelengths ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$) are coupled onto a single optical fiber using an optical prism or arrayed waveguide grating (AWG).

Coarse WDM (CWDM) vs. Dense WDM (DWDM)

  • CWDM (ITU-T G.694.2): Up to 18 channels with wide channel spacing ($20\ \text{nm}$). Inexpensive, uncooled lasers; used in metropolitan campus networks.
  • Dense WDM (DWDM — ITU-T G.694.1): Ultra-narrow channel spacing ($0.8\ \text{nm}$ or $0.4\ \text{nm}$, corresponding to $100\ \text{GHz}$ or $50\ \text{GHz}$ optical frequency grid). Multiplexes 80 to 160+ independent wavelengths on a single strand of fiber, delivering aggregate capacities exceeding $20 - 40\ \text{Tbps}$ across transoceanic undersea cables.

7.13 Why Switching? (Mesh Scalability Failure)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Switching Fundamentals — Need for Switched Networks
Switched Network — First-Principles Definition
A switched network consists of a series of interconnected nodes, called switches, capable of temporarily connecting two or more communicating end-devices on demand to transfer data, avoiding the catastrophic cost and physical impossibility of full point-to-point mesh connectivity.

The $N(N-1)/2$ Point-to-Point Collapse

To interconnect $N$ computers directly with point-to-point physical links requires:

$$L = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} \text{ links}$$

For a small village of $10,000$ telephones, direct point-to-point mesh cabling would require $\approx 50,000,000$ cables running across every street! By placing a central telephone switch in town, each telephone needs only 1 physical wire to the switch (total $10,000$ wires). Switching reduces physical complexity from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N)$.

7.14 Circuit Switching (Dedicated Resource Allocation)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Switching Techniques — Circuit Switching (Phases, Guarantees & Inefficiencies)
Circuit Switching — Definition
Circuit switching is a connection-oriented switching paradigm in which a dedicated, physical end-to-end transmission path is established through intermediate switching nodes before data transmission begins. The allocated resources (bandwidth, switch time slots, buffer space) remain exclusively reserved for that connection until explicit teardown.

The 3 Chronological Phases of Circuit Switching

  1. Connection Establishment (Setup Phase): A signaling probe traverses the network from source to destination. Intermediate switches allocate and bind physical circuits/time-slots. A confirmation signal returns to the sender.
  2. Data Transfer Phase: Bits flow continuously over the dedicated circuit. Because resources are reserved, data experiences zero queuing delay; the only latency is propagation delay ($t_{\text{prop}} = d / v$).
  3. Connection Teardown Phase: An explicit release signal deallocates switches and frees circuits for other subscribers.

Critical Advantages & Severe Inefficiencies

  • Guaranteed QoS: Continuous, constant data rate with predictable latency and zero jitter. Ideal for real-time voice phone calls (PSTN).
  • Massive Inefficiency for Bursty Computer Data: Computer communications (web browsing, coding, database queries) are bursty: a host transmits for 200 ms and remains idle for 10 seconds while a human reads. In circuit switching, the allocated bandwidth sits 100% idle and wasted during pauses; other users are blocked even though the line is silent.

7.15 Packet Switching (Store-and-Forward Pipelining)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Switching Techniques — Packet Switching (Store-and-Forward & Pipelining)
Packet Switching — Definition
Packet switching is a communications method where long messages are partitioned into small, discrete, bounded data units called packets. Packets traverse the network independently via intermediate packet switches (routers) using the store-and-forward principle.

Store-and-Forward Principle

A packet switch must receive the entire packet into its memory buffer and verify its integrity (FCS check) before it can begin transmitting the first bit of that packet onto the outgoing link.

The Pipelining Latency Reduction Derivation

Consider a message of $M$ bits transmitted over a path of $N$ hops, where each link operates at transmission rate $R$ bps (ignoring propagation and queuing delays):

  • Unpacketized Message Switching: The entire message of $M$ bits must be fully received by Hop 1, then Hop 2, ..., Hop $N$: $$T_{\text{message}} = N \times \frac{M}{R}$$
  • Packet Switching: The message is broken into $k$ small packets of length $L$ bits ($M = k \times L$). As soon as Packet 1 arrives at Switch 1, Switch 1 immediately begins transmitting Packet 1 to Switch 2, while the source simultaneously begins transmitting Packet 2 to Switch 1! $$T_{\text{packet}} = (N + k - 1) \times \frac{L}{R}$$
Numerical Proof of Packet Switching Pipelining Gain

   Parameters: File Size M = 10,000,000 bits (10 Mb).
   Path: N = 5 hops (4 intermediate routers). Link Speed R = 10 Mbps.

   CASE 1: MESSAGE SWITCHING (No Pipelining)
   Transmission time per hop = 10 Mb / 10 Mbps = 1.0 second.
   Total Time = 5 hops × 1.0 s = 5.0 SECONDS!

   CASE 2: PACKET SWITCHING (k = 1,000 packets of L = 10,000 bits)
   Transmission time per packet L / R = 10,000 bits / 10,000,000 bps = 0.001 s (1 ms).
   Total Time = (N + k - 1) × (L / R) = (5 + 1000 - 1) × 0.001 s = 1.004 SECONDS!

   RESULT: Packet switching is FIVE TIMES FASTER because links work in parallel (pipelining)!

7.16 Datagram vs. Virtual Circuit Networks

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Packet Switching Paradigms — Datagram (IP) vs Virtual Circuit (ATM/MPLS)
The Two Packet Switching Philosophies
  • Datagram Approach (Connectionless): Every packet is treated completely independently. Each packet carries the full source and destination IP address. Switches (routers) route packets individually. Packets belonging to the same message may traverse completely different physical paths and arrive out of order. (The fundamental design of the Internet Protocol / IP).
  • Virtual Circuit Approach (Connection-Oriented): A logical end-to-end path is established before sending data. All packets belonging to that flow carry a short Virtual Circuit Identifier (VCI) and follow the exact same sequence of switches, arriving in order. Switches maintain state tables mapping incoming VCIs to outgoing ports and new VCIs. (ATM, X.25, Frame Relay, MPLS).

Master Comparison: Datagram vs. Virtual Circuit

Dimension Datagram Network (Internet / IP) Virtual Circuit Network (ATM / MPLS)
Connection Setup None. Transmission begins immediately. Required. Must exchange setup signaling before sending data.
Addressing in Packets Full global addresses (32-bit IPv4 / 128-bit IPv6). Short local Virtual Circuit Identifier (VCI).
Router State Stateless. Routers maintain no per-connection state. Stateful. Routers maintain VCI mapping tables for active calls.
Packet Ordering Packets may arrive out of order. Packets always arrive in strict sequential order.
Router Failure Impact Highly robust. Packets dynamically route around failed nodes. Vulnerable. All VCs traversing the failed switch collapse instantly.
Quality of Service (QoS) Difficult (Best-Effort model). Easy (Bandwidth and buffers reserved during setup).

7.17 Message Switching (Historical Context)

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Switching History — Message Switching & Storage Limitations
Message Switching — Definition
Message switching is an archaic switching technique where messages are transmitted in their entirety as a single block. Intermediate switching nodes receive the complete message, store it temporarily on secondary storage (magnetic tape or hard disk), inspect the destination address, wait for an outgoing line to become free, and forward the entire message to the next switch.

Why Message Switching was Abandoned

  • Immense Storage Demands: Switches required massive, expensive disk drives to buffer multi-megabyte messages waiting for busy links.
  • Intolerable Latency: A 10 MB message traversing 5 switches at 1 Mbps took over 50 seconds, making interactive applications impossible.
  • Head-of-Line Blocking: A huge message monopolized a communication line for minutes, starving small urgent messages.

7.19 Summary & High-Yield Exam Traps

🎓 Uni Required Prescribed syllabus topic: Chapter 7 Revision Checklist & Common Pitfalls

Five Critical University Exam Traps

Trap ID Common Misconception First-Principles Reality & Refutation
Trap 1 "QPSK transmits at double the baud rate of BPSK." False! QPSK operates at the exact same baud rate as BPSK, but conveys double the bit rate ($2\ \text{bits/symbol}$).
Trap 2 "Circuit switching has higher data transmission latency than packet switching." False! Once established, circuit switching has zero queuing delay and lower transfer latency than store-and-forward packet switching. Its latency penalty is strictly in the initial setup phase.
Trap 3 "In datagram networks, routers guarantee packet delivery." False! Datagram IP networks provide Best-Effort delivery; packets may be dropped under congestion. Reliability is handled by Layer 4 (TCP).
Trap 4 "Synchronous TDM requires packet destination addresses in each slot." False! Synchronous TDM requires no addressing because the position of the time slot implicitly identifies the sender. Only Statistical TDM requires addressing headers!
Trap 5 "WDM is a time-division multiplexing technique." False! WDM is strictly an analog Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM) technique operating in the optical electromagnetic spectrum.
